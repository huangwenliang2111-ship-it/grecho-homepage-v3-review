What Should Be Frozen Before Two Board Trials Are Compared?

Before comparing two facer trials, agree on the decision the comparison is meant to support, identify the construction and process used for each run, and keep the measurement conditions consistent. Record unavoidable changes separately. A difference between two finished boards is not automatically a difference caused by the facer.

A trial can produce good boards and still produce a weak comparison. The problem usually becomes visible at the review table: one sample has a clear production history; the other has a result, a photograph and the description “same as before”. There is no reliable way to reconstruct what “same” included.

For a GRECHO sample-review discussion, we would start with that missing comparison record, not ask for another batch of measurements immediately. The useful question is not simply which board performed better. It is whether the evidence answers the question that justified making the boards.

Two legitimate questions that need different trial plans

Consider an illustrative planning situation, not a reported GRECHO customer trial. A manufacturer is comparing its reference facer with a candidate. The candidate is run with a different adhesive setting because that produces a more acceptable surface.

Neither question is wrong. The mistake is running the second comparison and reporting the answer as though the first had been tested.

If safe operation requires a process change, make it and record it. Do not keep an unsuitable setting merely to preserve the appearance of a controlled experiment. Instead, describe the trial honestly as a construction-and-process comparison.

This distinction also helps separate an initial sample suitability review from a comparative trial. The former asks whether a direction deserves evaluation. The latter asks what the observed difference can be attributed to.

“Freeze” means define and record, not pretend nothing varies

Some inputs can be held constant. Others can only be kept within an agreed range. Still others, such as the production day or a raw-material batch, may change despite careful planning.

Use three labels in the trial record: deliberately changed, controlled, and recorded but not controlled. A blank cell is not a fourth category meaning “probably unchanged”. Mark it unknown.

Keep time from becoming the hidden explanation

Running all reference material on one day and all candidate material on another can entangle the material change with a day change. NIST's experimental-design guidance treats nuisance factors separately from the factor being investigated and describes blocking as a way to organise comparisons around them.[1]

The practical lesson is modest: when planning allows, place meaningful reference and candidate comparisons within comparable production periods rather than letting material identity and operating period become inseparable.

A returning reference can be useful. If the reference looks different at the end of the session, the candidate should not be judged against the start-of-session reference as though the baseline were unquestionably stable. This is an indication to investigate drift, not a mathematical correction that automatically rescues the trial.

Randomisation, repeated runs and blocking should be designed around the equipment and the safety constraints. Where changes are difficult or costly, restricted randomisation needs to be recognised rather than hidden; NIST discusses such arrangements separately.[2] Do not repeatedly switch a production line in an unsafe or technically inappropriate way just to imitate a laboratory schedule.

Five pieces from one board do not represent five production runs

Suppose five test pieces are cut from one board. They can reveal differences across that board and variability in specimen preparation. They do not, simply by being five pieces, demonstrate reproducibility across five independent production runs.

Keep both levels visible: how many boards or runs were made, and how many specimens were cut from each. Combining the results into one average can conceal the distinction. NIST's discussion of nested variation is relevant here: observations can be grouped within a larger experimental unit, and their structure matters to interpretation.[2]

For a first practical review, retain the individual results with their sample positions. A mean without the underlying pattern may disguise a good centre and weak edges, or one favourable board among several inconsistent ones. Deciding the required sample size and statistical analysis belongs with the competent trial and testing team; this article does not prescribe a minimum number.

Agree on the release decision before seeing the best sample

A useful trial should not leave the team with only “better” or “worse”. It should support a defined next step: reject the direction, repeat a limited comparison, extend a promising result, or release a specifically described construction within an agreed scope.

Set the primary requirement before running. Keep secondary requirements visible, too. A stronger interface may not compensate for an unacceptable surface; a cleaner surface may not answer a board-performance requirement. Which trade-offs are acceptable is a project decision, not something a supplier can infer from the most favourable measurement.

A good closing note might read: “The candidate-plus-process construction met the agreed screening checks. A facer-only comparison was not performed. Further production verification is required before extending the result to other conditions.” That is a useful conclusion, even without a dramatic improvement percentage.

For GRECHO's part in application-matched facer selection, the most helpful request includes the decision to be made, the two construction records, the change ledger and the results still needing explanation. Sample and documentation coordination can then follow the actual uncertainty.

Questions to settle before comparing results

Must every variable be identical?

No. The factor under investigation must differ, and some process changes may be necessary. The important step is to identify them and limit the conclusion accordingly. A changed facer plus a changed process is a package comparison unless a suitable design separates their effects.

Can the previous month's accepted board be the reference?

It can provide context, but it is not automatically a matched experimental control. Differences in raw materials, conditioning, production period and test preparation need to be considered before attributing a result to the new facer.

Does one successful board justify a production release?

It can justify a next step, but it does not by itself define the breadth of release. The trial owner should specify which construction, process range and verification evidence the decision covers.
