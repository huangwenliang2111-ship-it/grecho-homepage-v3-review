Why Can an Ultra-Low-Airflow Facer Still Fail a PIR/PUR Foam-Board Trial?

A very low air-permeability result answers a question about the tested facer, not whether a particular foam-board process will produce an acceptable interface and finished board. Foam-system identity, contact conditions, line history and the timing and method of bond evaluation still matter. Low airflow is a screening input, not a release criterion for the board.

There is also a naming problem to remove at the outset: “ultra-low” is not a universal pass threshold in this article. It describes a purchaser's or supplier's low-airflow material direction. The value, method and conditions still need to be stated before that description becomes useful.

The practical question is what to do when the dry-facer result looks satisfactory but the board trial does not. Rejecting the result as irrelevant would be a mistake. Treating it as proof that the rest of the process must work would be another.

Put the airflow result in the right place

Start with the measurement basis: method, pressure difference, specimen state, tested face where relevant, and the conditions used to report the result. ISO 9237 concerns fabric permeability to air; it is not a foam-board bonding or process-qualification method.[1]

Our air-permeability review for PIR/PUR facers covers material-screening questions. This article addresses the next decision: what must be reviewed when the selected material enters a specific foam process.

It is useful to retain two separate statements: “the facer met the agreed airflow screening criterion” and “the board trial met, or did not meet, its agreed requirements”. One can be true while the other is false. Combining them into “the facer passed” hides which claim has actually been checked.

A reacting foam process has a time history

PIMA's manufacturing description places the facing within a sequence: mixed ingredients contact a facer, the reacting material enters the laminator, and the board develops through that process. It identifies the laminator as relevant to thickness, cell formation, curing and facer adhesion.[2] This is public industry information about polyiso manufacturing, not a record of a GRECHO line trial.

The relevant implication is that a dry sheet test cannot capture that whole sequence. Record the foam system and its approved processing requirements, the facing contact arrangement, relevant operating conditions, and the board's state when it is sampled and assessed.

Do not assume that a PIR system and a PUR system with similar visual output have identical processing behaviour. Nor should an air-barrier target be treated as permission to adjust formulation or equipment outside approved limits. The foam supplier and the competent production team should direct those decisions.

Line speed deserves more than a note saying “faster”

A simple geometry example shows why the time record matters. These figures are hypothetical, not GRECHO production settings or recommendations.

The calculation is distance divided by speed. Under those assumptions, the second run spends 25% less time in that length. It does not calculate chemical cure, prove a bonding problem, or establish a suitable speed for any machine.

This is exactly why the calculation belongs in a review rather than a recipe. If a board is sampled at the same physical point after a speed change, its processing history may not be comparable. “Same sampling station” and “same process time” are not interchangeable statements.

The useful record is the actual relevant length, speed and timing, together with the foam supplier's and equipment supplier's defined operating conditions. Do not estimate cure from distance alone.

When a separation has already occurred, preserve both surfaces and their relationship to the board. The existing facer-to-foam failure-diagnosis guide addresses failure observations in detail. Repeating that diagnosis is not the purpose here; this review checks whether the process comparison can support it.

A passing result can still be too narrow to transfer

Consider an illustrative scenario. A candidate gives an acceptable result with one identified foam system and processing arrangement. A later run changes the foam system or speed, and the finished board is less satisfactory. No GRECHO trial or customer result is implied.

The first result has not become meaningless. Its scope has become important. It supports the construction and conditions actually evaluated, not every process with the same material name.

For a follow-up, keep the accepted combination as an identified reference. Define which change is being evaluated and what the plant can safely control. Record necessary adjustments rather than hiding them. If the process has to change together with the material, report a package comparison, not a facer-only effect.

Do not describe an improvement after an adjustment as a proven mechanism unless the comparison actually isolates it. An association can justify another targeted trial without yet explaining the cause.

What would justify a release decision?

An airflow result is one line in the evidence record. A process-specific decision should also identify the board construction, foam-system reference, relevant operating conditions, specimen state, applicable tests and the limits of the evaluation.

The review should answer three practical questions. Can the favourable result be reproduced within the intended process? Are the required board properties covered by the evidence? What change would trigger another review?

There is no universal peel force or process speed offered here. The relevant acceptance conditions belong to the specified board and the applicable test or customer requirement. Fire, thermal and other finished-board claims also need their own evidence; they cannot be read from the loose-facer airflow value.

For a PIR/PUR facer discussion, GRECHO can coordinate candidate material and documentation review. Bring the reference construction and the changed-run record, not just the lowest value from a datasheet. Where formulation details are confidential, agree on an appropriate technical exchange rather than putting a proprietary recipe into a general enquiry form.

Questions behind a low-airflow selection

Does lower airflow mean a stronger foam bond?

Not by itself. An air-permeability measurement does not measure bond strength or establish compatibility with a particular foam system and process. Keep the airflow screening and board/interface evaluation separate.

Can the same facer be transferred to another foam formulation without review?

A previous result provides context, but it does not automatically cover a different formulation or processing history. Define the new combination and the evidence needed to evaluate it.

If the board separates, should the facer be replaced immediately?

Preserve the specimen and process record first. A replacement may be appropriate later, but the initial observation does not isolate the facer, the foam system, contact conditions or the evaluation method as the cause.

Discuss the foam-board trial record with GRECHO.
