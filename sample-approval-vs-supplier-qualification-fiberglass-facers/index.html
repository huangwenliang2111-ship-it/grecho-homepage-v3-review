Sample Approval vs Supplier Qualification: What Still Needs to Be Verified Before a Fiberglass Facer Enters Production?

A positive sample result is one decision, not a complete supplier approval. Track technical evaluation, supplier qualification and commercial approval separately.

A passed fiberglass facer sample closes only the evaluation and scope recorded. It does not automatically approve the supplying company, authorize production use or establish an order. Keep technical evaluation, supplier qualification and commercial approval separate, then confirm what the sample decision allows next and which approvals remain open.

For a board manufacturer, “the sample passed” is useful progress—but an incomplete handoff. Did the team approve the appearance of a loose sheet, the result of a bonded specimen, or a defined board-line trial? Has purchasing approved the supplier for the relevant category? Is the next quantity for testing, a trial order or routine supply?

This guide addresses the decision after a positive sample evaluation. It proposes a three-track record for coordinating technical, quality and purchasing teams. It is not a universal onboarding sequence: a customer’s reviews may run in parallel, return to an earlier question or omit steps that do not apply.

1. Record what the sample approval actually covers

Before interpreting the word “approved”, attach it to a specific object and decision. Record the facer reference and revision, sample or roll identification where available, the evaluated construction, the criterion used, the result and the person authorized to approve the next step.

For example, “appearance accepted for the identified acoustic-panel sample” is more precise than “material approved”. It does not silently include runnability, bonding under different conditions or finished-panel performance that was not evaluated.

Keep feedback and authorization distinct. A positive observation from an engineer may be valuable without being the organization’s formal release for a production trial. Ask what the feedback authorizes rather than inferring authority from a job title.

For preparation and evaluation before this point, use the sample-review checklist for board manufacturers. Here, the task is to interpret the recorded outcome without expanding its scope.

2. Track technical evaluation, supplier qualification and commercial approval separately

Use three parallel records instead of one “approved/not approved” label. The table is a suggested coordination model, not a description of every manufacturer’s process.

Track Decision to record Evidence to request or retain
Technical evaluation What has the identified material or construction demonstrated, and what evaluation may happen next? Sample identity, method or assessment basis, conditions, result, limitations and the authorized next step.
Supplier qualification Has the supplying organization met this customer’s applicable category and organizational approval requirements? The customer’s requirements, review status, unresolved actions and approval record with its stated scope.
Commercial approval What quantity, specification and transaction have actually been agreed or authorized? Quotation/RFQ status, agreed specification revision, quantity basis, delivery conditions and any order/acceptance record.

A practical update might read: technical—appearance review passed; supplier qualification—review pending; commercial—budget quotation requested. That is more actionable than describing the whole project as approved.

These records need not advance at the same speed. A budget discussion can help determine whether further evaluation is worthwhile. Supplier documentation can be reviewed while samples are being assessed. Neither action supplies a missing technical result.

3. Do not treat registration as the supplier-approval decision

An online account and a completed supplier profile are not sufficient evidence of final approval. In its published supplier information, ROCKWOOL describes completed registration as a Pre-Qualified status pending approval by a Group Sourcing and Procurement Category Manager. Its stated final assessment includes requirements, responsible sourcing and market analysis. [1]

That example illustrates a distinction, not a status that should be assigned to every registered supplier. Read the customer’s actual approval record and terminology. Portal access, a reference number or a confirmation email should not be relabelled as a purchasing decision.

Supplier review can also address issues outside the sample. Saint-Gobain’s Responsible Purchasing description includes environmental, social and ethical considerations in its purchasing process. [2] Its published Responsible Development Clause, marked as updated in June 2020, describes risk-based supplier analysis and, where needed, document reviews or on-site audits, followed by corrective measures when gaps are identified. [3]

These are company-specific public examples. They do not prescribe the same audit for every facer supplier, and they are not evidence of a GRECHO supply relationship, approval or current purchasing project.

For your project, ask which requirements apply to the supplying entity, material category and intended using site, and who confirms completion. An organization-level review and a product/sample result should remain separate records. Use the technical-document request guide to agree the appropriate document scope rather than forwarding an unrestricted file pack.

4. Separate a quotation, a trial order and routine supply

Treat the commercial record as its own source of evidence. An RFQ describes a request for quotation; it does not by itself establish an accepted order. Likewise, a forecast should not be recorded as a committed purchase quantity.

For the next transaction, confirm the material and specification revision, quantity type, unit of measure, delivery location and the conditions still under discussion. Distinguish a sample quantity, a trial quantity, an annual forecast and an actual order rather than combining them into one volume figure.

A trial order may have a deliberately limited purpose. Record what it covers and the review expected after delivery. Do not describe it as routine-supply approval or guaranteed repeat business unless the applicable agreement and approval records support that description.

This is a recordkeeping distinction, not a legal interpretation of a contract. The responsible commercial parties determine what has been agreed and what authorization is still needed.

5. Define what may proceed after the positive result

The useful question is not simply “What is still missing?” It is “Which next action is supported now, and who can authorize it?” A limited approval can move the project forward without implying that every other review is complete.

Use the following handoff checklist as a starting point. Each next step is subject to the customer’s process and actual authorization.

Decision being recorded Next step to confirm What must not be assumed Responsible role to confirm
Sample evaluation passed within a stated scope Select the candidate for the next defined evaluation. All other properties, production use and supplier qualification are approved. Technical reviewer and next-stage approver.
Controlled board-line trial reviewed Decide whether to extend evaluation, resolve a remaining issue or consider a limited order. The result applies to every line, site, construction or future lot. Production/technical lead and quality reviewer.
Supplier approval recorded Confirm the category, entity and scope for which purchasing may proceed. An order, exclusive appointment or purchase volume has been awarded. Purchasing/category owner and relevant approval function.
Trial order agreed Deliver and review the specified trial quantity under the agreed conditions. Routine supply or future orders are already committed. Purchasing, planning and quality contacts.
Routine supply authorized in a defined scope Supply against the applicable orders, specification and agreed controls. Later material or process changes fall automatically within that scope. Purchasing and designated quality/change owners.

Hypothetical example: an acoustic-facing sample passes its appearance review

A board-development team accepts the visible finish of a white fiberglass facer on the identified sample. The process team has not yet evaluated the material as a roll on its intended line. Supplier onboarding is still under review, and purchasing has requested only an indicative quotation.

The useful outcome is not “supplier approved”. It is a documented appearance decision, an agreed next technical evaluation, a separate onboarding status and a clearly labelled commercial discussion.

This scenario is an editorial teaching example, not a GRECHO customer case or test result. It also does not imply that every project must use the same sequence. For the technical design of a subsequent comparison, refer to the controlled board-trial comparison guide.

6. Keep technical concerns visible when purchasing moves ahead

A project can have a genuine purchasing objective while a technical issue remains unresolved. Do not erase the issue because an RFQ exists, or erase the commercial context because one evaluation did not pass.

Record the affected sample, criterion and unresolved result, then ask the responsible team to define the next review. A limited investigative step may be appropriate, but it should not be described as production release against a criterion that has not been met.

Conversely, where the technical result is positive but onboarding is incomplete, focus on the outstanding organizational decision. Repeating the same sample test will not necessarily answer a supplier-approval question.

A concise handoff can say: “The stated sample evaluation is complete. Supplier approval remains pending. Please confirm the applicable category review, the outstanding evidence and the person responsible for authorizing the next step.” Adapt that wording to the actual record; it is not a substitute for the customer’s decision.

7. Agree how changes will be reviewed

Before treating approval as ongoing, record the material reference, specification revision and relevant construction/process scope to which it applies. Ask the customer which changes require notification or review and who makes that decision.

Potential review questions include a changed facer construction, coating or reinforcement; a revised supplied specification; a different intended contact side or assembly; or a change in the relevant processing or supply arrangement. These are prompts for a change discussion—not a declaration that every change requires a full retest.

Where a change affects an existing report, keep the report-applicability review separate from the supplier’s organizational approval. Our guide to facer reports and finished-board performance addresses that evidence boundary. This article does not replace it.

A useful closing record answers five questions: what was approved, for which scope, on what evidence, by whom, and what action is now permitted? Keep any remaining technical, supplier and commercial conditions alongside that record.

Frequently asked questions

Is sample approval the same as supplier qualification?

No. In this framework, sample approval records an evaluation of an identified material or construction; supplier qualification records the customer’s approval of the supplying organization within an applicable scope. A company may coordinate these reviews, but one record should not be used to invent the other. ROCKWOOL’s published registration and final-approval distinction provides one company-specific example. [1]

Does “Pre-Qualified” mean the supplier can receive orders?

Do not decide from the label alone. Check the customer’s definition and the actual approval/ordering status. ROCKWOOL, for example, uses Pre-Qualified for the stage before Category Manager approval; that wording should not be generalized to another organization’s system. [1]

Can pricing be discussed before every technical review is complete?

In the proposed three-track model, yes: label the discussion as preliminary and record its assumptions. Keep a budget quotation or RFQ separate from technical acceptance, supplier approval and an agreed order. Follow the customer’s actual procedure rather than imposing a fixed sequence.

Does a passed sample approve all later lots?

Not automatically. The sample decision has a defined object and scope. Confirm the agreed incoming checks, ongoing requirements and change-review process before extending that decision to later supply. Do not assume that every future lot needs an identical trial either; the applicable controls must be agreed.
