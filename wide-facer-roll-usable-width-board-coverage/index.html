Wide Facer Rolls: Specify the Width That Reaches the Board

A wide roll is useful only when enough acceptable material reaches the part of the board that must be covered. Nominal roll width, minimum delivered width, usable coated width and finished board width answer different questions. Our recommendation is to put them on one drawing before agreeing a trial roll.

This distinction matters to GRECHO’s Europe Wide-Width Facer Initiative. The initiative collects application-specific material requirements and coordinates their review with selected supply-chain partners.[1] It is not evidence that every requested width is available, or that a particular production route has already been qualified.

The worked example below shows how we would clarify a width request. All dimensions are illustrative. They are not GRECHO manufacturing limits, stock sizes, tolerances or customer results.

A 1,200 mm board does not automatically need a 1,200 mm roll

Suppose the finished board is described as 1,200 mm wide. A buyer asks for a roll “a little wider than that” and receives a nominal 1,250 mm offer. The headline difference is 50 mm, which sounds comfortable.

Now add the missing definitions. The board may reach 1,202 mm at the relevant covering stage. Some material near each roll edge may be outside the accepted coating zone. The web can also move relative to the board during the process. These are separate allowances, and all of them consume space.

None should be invented by the supplier to make the calculation look safe. The customer must confirm the board geometry and process envelope; the material route must confirm what width and edge condition it can actually supply.

The useful question is not “Is 1,250 greater than 1,200?” It is “Does the minimum acceptable coverage envelope still fit at the least favourable agreed condition?”

Put four widths on the drawing

Width What it means in the review Who needs to confirm it
Nominal delivered width The stated width of the supplied roll Material supplier and procurement
Minimum delivered width The smallest width accepted under the agreed tolerance Material supplier and quality
Usable material width The continuous zone meeting the agreed facing or coating requirements Material supplier, quality and process team
Required coverage width The board area and process allowance that the usable zone must cover Board manufacturer and process team

Do not double-count the edge deduction. If a supplier already specifies a minimum usable coated width, do not subtract the same nonconforming edge zones again. Conversely, a gross web width should not silently be treated as fully coated usable width.

Also identify the measurement stage. A width taken from an unloaded roll sample is not automatically the width recorded under the conditions used on the converting line. The specification should say how and where it is measured, without assuming that tension produces a predictable change.

The width check, with the assumptions visible

For the illustrative request, use these agreed inputs:

Input Illustrative value
Nominal delivered width 1,250 mm
Minimum delivered width 1,246 mm
Unusable edge zone, each side 12 mm
Maximum board width requiring coverage 1,202 mm
Required lateral allowance, each side 8 mm

On a simplified centred layout:

Minimum usable width = 1,246 − 12 − 12 = 1,222 mm.

Required coverage width = 1,202 + 8 + 8 = 1,218 mm.

Remaining width margin = 1,222 − 1,218 = 4 mm.

The initial 50 mm impression has become a 4 mm margin in this stated model. That does not prove the arrangement is acceptable. It tells the team where the decision now sits: are the inputs verified, are other allowances missing, and is that remaining margin adequate for the agreed process?

The centred assumption matters. An offset coating zone, uneven edge deductions or asymmetric tracking limits must be shown on the drawing. A positive total-width calculation can still hide a shortfall on one side. Edge wrapping, overlaps or a requirement to cover a profiled section also need their own geometry; they are not included in this example.

More width is not always the next answer

If the coverage margin is insufficient, a wider roll is one possible response. Another is a better-defined usable coating zone, a revised cutting plan or a narrower process allowance that the customer can demonstrate reliably. These are review options, not promises that the material or equipment can accommodate them.

A wider roll should also be checked against the unwind and downstream handling limits. Confirm the acceptable core, roll diameter, roll mass, handling method and any relevant packaging constraints with the equipment and logistics owners. A roll that covers the board on paper is not yet a line-ready roll.

At GRECHO, we would keep “required by the application” separate from “available from the selected route” until both sides have been confirmed. That prevents an application target from turning into an unsupported supply claim.

Compare material use without inventing a savings figure

For a simple single-lane example with a 1,200 mm finished width from a 1,250 mm input web, the width-only utilisation is:

1,200 ÷ 1,250 = 96%.

The corresponding width difference is 4% of the input width. This calculation is not the total production yield. It excludes starts and stops, joints, rejected sections, length losses, other cutting layouts and changes in run conditions.

It also does not prove that a narrower roll is cheaper overall. A narrower web could reduce nominal trim while leaving less room for the actual process. Compare the material cost per accepted board only after the trial records show usable output. Do not quote a projected waste reduction as an achieved customer saving.

For multi-lane cutting, draw the lanes, inter-lane losses and outer edge zones explicitly. Multiplying the nominal board width by the number of lanes is only the beginning of that layout.

What we would ask for before coordinating a trial

One annotated cross-width sketch is usually more useful than a long email containing several different width numbers. Mark the board edges, acceptable facer zone, coating boundaries, intended direction and the stage at which each width applies. Add the required roll format and the proposed trial objective.

The objective should be concrete: for example, checking coverage over the agreed process envelope, or confirming whether a selected surface remains acceptable across the usable zone. Neither objective is equivalent to full commercial qualification.

During the trial, retain the actual measured widths and record where the usable boundaries sit. The incoming-roll inspection guide addresses receiving checks; the lot and roll traceability guide addresses identity. This article supplies the missing dimensional agreement between those steps and the finished board.

The review should end with a width definition that both teams can use, not merely a larger number on a quotation.

Use one drawing revision for the width handover

Before the trial roll is agreed, put the supplier’s width definition and the board team’s coverage definition on the same drawing revision. A short handover note should make the following distinctions explicit.

Drawing item What to mark
Reference edge and orientation Identify left and right consistently, the machine direction and the intended contact face.
Delivered and usable boundaries Show the minimum delivered width and the agreed usable coated zone separately. State which edge exclusions are already included.
Required coverage Show the board area that must remain covered and the allowance already assigned to each side.
Position, not only total width Locate the usable zone relative to the board. A total-width surplus does not, by itself, prove acceptable coverage at both edges.
Measurement stage State whether dimensions refer to the received roll, the trial setup or a later converting stage.
Revision and review owner Keep the drawing revision with the roll request and identify who resolves a difference between the supplied material and the trial requirement.

This handover record is a review aid, not a new dimensional tolerance or an additional edge deduction. Keep the agreed geometry intact when transferring it from the request to the trial record.

Once the width requirement is clear, use the wide-width facer trial readiness checklist to align roll identity, equipment limits, comparison conditions and the acceptance record. Width agreement is one input to that wider review, not a substitute for it.

Questions that should be settled in the specification

Does “wide-width” mean fully usable coating from edge to edge?

Not unless the agreed specification says so. Define the usable zone and the edge condition separately from nominal roll width. Do not infer full-width coating quality from the headline width alone.

Can a small hand sample establish full-width process suitability?

It can support a material or surface review. It does not establish tracking, across-width consistency or handling of the proposed roll format. Those questions need representative evidence and an agreed trial scope.

Can GRECHO confirm a requested width immediately?

Width feasibility depends on the selected material direction, process, project conditions and available supply resources. GRECHO’s role is to coordinate that review; a request is not a supply commitment.[1][2]

Share your board application and usable-width requirement with the Europe Wide-Width Facer Initiative. Add the drawing inputs you already know; detailed technical information can follow through the agreed review route.
